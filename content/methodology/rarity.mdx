# Reason about infrastructure rarity

Rarity helps order an investigation; it does not establish maliciousness. Infrastructure is relatively stable, so a first-seen device role or dependency can be valuable even when the same protocol is common elsewhere. Do not implement a universal numerical score: retain the factors and the evidence so an analyst can explain the priority.

## Conceptual Infrastructure Hunt Score

Consider the following factors together:

1. **Origin rarity** — has this appliance or device class ever initiated traffic of this kind?
2. **Direction and role rarity** — is the appliance unexpectedly a client, scanner, proxy, resolver, file-transfer source, or tunnel endpoint?
3. **Destination rarity** — is the peer new to the device, site, role, organization, or threat-intelligence context?
4. **Protocol and port rarity** — is the service absent from the allow matrix, carried on an unusual port, or used in an unexpected security mode?
5. **Time and cadence rarity** — did it occur outside maintenance, recur at machine-like intervals, or begin after a long quiet period?
6. **Volume and duration rarity** — are bytes, packets, session length, or transfer direction unusual for this dependency?
7. **Fan-out and peer rarity** — did one appliance contact many systems, or did many appliances converge on one new destination?
8. **Change rarity** — did a route, ACL, resolver, account, capture, logging target, image, process, or tunnel state change without an approved record?
9. **Sequence rarity** — did individually explainable events form a suspicious chain, such as capture then transfer, or telemetry loss then a new tunnel?
10. **Independent corroboration** — do upstream flow, passive sensors, AAA, configuration history, or adjacent controls confirm or contradict the device's account?

The conceptual relationship combines these evidence terms:

  <li><strong>Signal:</strong> unusual origin</li>
  <li><strong>Signal:</strong> unusual role or direction</li>
  <li><strong>Signal:</strong> rare dependency</li>
  <li><strong>Signal:</strong> suspicious sequence</li>
  <li><strong>Confidence modifier:</strong> independent corroboration</li>
  <li><strong>Outcome:</strong> higher hunt priority</li>
</ul>

## Compare at the right scope

Use several reference groups. Compare the device with its own history, same-role peers, the same site, the approved dependency inventory, and the current change window. A behavior can be frequent globally yet unprecedented for one router; conversely, a first-seen connection may be an approved rollout affecting an entire device class.

## Preserve reasons, not just ranks

Write the factors into the case: “first device-originated SSH, new external destination, no change ticket, repeated every five minutes, confirmed by upstream IPFIX.” That statement survives threshold changes and gives the investigator a concrete hypothesis. A bare score does neither.

## Keep the denominator visible

The following arithmetic is synthetic, not a measured detector result. Suppose 100,000 events contain 100 malicious events. A detector that finds 90 of those and flags 1% of the 99,900 benign events creates 90 true alerts and 999 false alerts. Only about 8.3% of its 1,089 alerts are true, despite detecting 90% of malicious events. Event-level results also differ from case-level results when many events belong to one incident.

Record both the number of candidates and the population searched. Do not call the fraction of reviewed alerts found malicious a detection rate: that excludes attacks the hunt missed. Recall requires an independently labeled set of relevant malicious activity; most exploratory hunts cannot estimate it from production hits alone.

## Validate ranking on later activity

[TESSERACT](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/pendlebury) demonstrates population and temporal bias in malware-classifier evaluation. Our methodological inference is to tune on one period and review a later, representative period, including benign operational changes. Keep related events from the same known incident together when separating development and evaluation data. These practices help expose overfitting; the paper does not validate this guide's rarity factors or numerical thresholds.

Record review workload, adjudicated case outcomes, known missed validation cases, and collection gaps separately. If you inspect only the highest-ranked candidates, label the resulting precision estimate as conditional on that selection. Revisit exclusions after role changes, migrations, and new telemetry rather than silently expanding an allowlist.

An ATT&CK mapping is a behavior label. It is not proof that every implementation of a technique is observable. [MITRE CAR](https://car.mitre.org/) explicitly separates the threat model from detection implementations and describes hypotheses, data models, pseudocode, and tests. For each deployed analytic, retain those details and the local validation result.
